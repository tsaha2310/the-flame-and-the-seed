The copier is DNA polymerase. It moves along a template strand and adds, one at a time, the nucleotide whose base pairs with the template's, joining it to the growing strand's end by the condensation from IV.7. It picks the right one most of the time because only the right one fits the width, from IV.8 L05.

When it picks wrong, the pair bulges or pinches, and the polymerase's own shape feels it. It backs up one letter, clips the wrong nucleotide off with a second pocket it carries, and adds again. Checking each pair and clipping a mismatch before moving on is proofreading. It catches ninety-nine in a hundred of the copier's slips, taking the rate from one in a hundred thousand to one in ten million.

Then a second check. Enzymes patrol the finished helix, feeling for bulges the copier missed, and cut out and refill the wrong stretch, reading the old strand to know which of the two letters was wrong. This is why the old strand is marked, and why DNA uses T instead of U: a damaged C turns into U, and any U in DNA is known to be an error, from IV.8 L02. The patrol takes the rate to about one in a billion.

**DNA polymerase copies by pairing and proofreads by feel, clipping a mismatch before it moves on. A repair patrol catches most of the rest. About one slip per billion letters survives.**

One in a billion, over three billion letters, is a few slips per copy of a human genome. Those few are the mutations from VI.4. They are the raw material of everything after this lesson.
