The pieces of a gene that spell recipe are exons, because they are expressed. The stretches between, copied and then thrown away, are introns, because they sit in between. A typical human gene has eight or nine introns, and they are often far longer than the exons.

Cutting out the introns and joining the exons is splicing. It is done by a machine built of small RNAs and proteins that recognises the short sequences at each intron's ends, from Lock and Shape, loops the intron out, cuts, and joins. The joins must land exactly, because a join off by one letter is a frameshift, from VI.4 L03.

Two more edits. A cap is put on the copy's front end, a modified nucleotide that the ribosome recognises as "a real message, start here". A tail of a hundred or so A's is added to the back, which protects the message from being cut too soon and sets how long it lives, from VI.2 L03. Only a capped, tailed, spliced message is escorted out through a pore.

**A eukaryotic gene is exons with introns between. The copy is spliced, introns out and exons joined exactly, capped and tailed, and only then leaves the nucleus.**
