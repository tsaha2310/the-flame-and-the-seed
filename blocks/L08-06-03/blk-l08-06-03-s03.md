The two trees agree on most of the big branches, which means Linnaeus's looks were mostly tracking descent. Both put every animal with a backbone, or the rod that comes before one in an embryo, into one group: chordates. Both put every chordate with fur and milk into one group: mammals. Both put flowering plants together, and ferns and mosses on earlier branches. Where a feature is inherited, looks and tape agree.

They disagree where a way of life reshaped a body. The looks-tree, sorting by teeth and limbs, puts whales in their own corner of the mammals. The DNA tree puts them beside hippos, inside the group of even-toed hoofed animals. A whale is a hoofed mammal whose line went back to the sea, and fossils found since, with legs and hooves and a whale's ear bones, agree with the tape. The looks-tree puts birds beside mammals as warm-blooded feathered things. The DNA tree puts them inside the reptiles, beside crocodiles, as the living branch of the dinosaurs. The tape sees what the eye could not.

Among plants: flowering plants, from the moong to a mango tree, are one group; conifers are earlier; ferns, with no seeds, earlier still; mosses, with no pipes from VII.5, earliest of the land plants.

**The looks-tree and the DNA tree agree wherever looks follow inheritance: chordates, mammals, flowering plants. They disagree where a way of life reshaped a body: whales are hoofed mammals gone to sea, and birds are living dinosaurs.**
