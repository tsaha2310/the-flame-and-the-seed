Sodium, magnesium, calcium: metals, on the left. Their oxides are ionic lattices, oxide ions held by metal ions. In water, the oxide ion grabs a hydrogen from a water molecule and becomes hydroxide. The water turns base. These are basic oxides.

Carbon, sulphur, phosphorus, nitrogen: non-metals, on the right. Their oxides are covalent molecules. In water they join with a water molecule and then let go of a hydrogen ion. Carbon dioxide gives carbonic acid, the fizz in soda. Sulphur dioxide gives sulphurous acid, the sting of a burning match. These are acidic oxides.

Aluminium, near the staircase, does both, depending on what it meets. Silicon's oxide, sand, does almost nothing in water at all.

The line between basic and acidic oxides is the same line that divides metals from non-metals in Book I. And it comes from the same Tug-of-War: a metal gives its electrons to oxygen wholly, and the resulting oxide ion is a base; a non-metal shares, and the resulting molecule releases hydrogen ions.

**Metal oxides are bases. Non-metal oxides are acids. The staircase in the periodic table is the border.**

Chuna on acid soil sweetens it: a basic oxide cancelling the soil's acid. The carbon dioxide from every fire and every breath makes rain, rivers and the sea slightly acid.
