Phosphorus is a non-metal, so its oxide is an acid, from III.7. In water that acid gives up its hydrogen ions and is left as a phosphate: a phosphorus with four oxygens, carrying negative charge.

Push two phosphates together and their negative charges repel. Joining them costs energy, from I.2's charge Hill. Push a third on and it costs more. A chain of three phosphates is three negatives crowded on a stick, and the stick is sitting high.

Let go, and the end phosphate flies off, taking a water molecule to fill its bond. The chain drops from three to two, and the energy comes out. With water absent, the sim shows the same drop but smaller: much of what comes out is the freed phosphate settling comfortably among water molecules that grip its charges.

So a chain of three phosphates is a stored fall. Not because any bond in it is special, but because the pieces sit far lower once they are apart in water.

**A phosphate is a charged group. A chain of three phosphates is crowded negatives sitting high on the Hill, and letting one go is a fall.**

Hold that sentence beside III.4's guardrail. Breaking any bond costs energy. The end phosphate pays out because the pieces settle into a much lower state in water, not because the bond was "high-energy".
