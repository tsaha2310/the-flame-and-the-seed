Inside the salt, every atom is an ion: sodium atoms that have lost their one outer electron, so they are positive, and chlorine atoms that have gained it, so they are negative. They sit in a checkerboard, every positive surrounded by negatives. Opposite charges close together, low on the Hill. That is what holds the crystal.

Push one row over by one place, and suddenly positives sit next to positives. Like charges repel. The two halves fly apart along that line. That is the crack.

Inside the copper, there are no ions in a checkerboard. The atoms sit in rows with their outer electrons loose between them, shared by everyone. Push a row along and every atom still has the same loose electrons around it. Nothing repels. It dents and holds.

Two questions come out of this. Why does sodium give away exactly one electron and chlorine take exactly one? And what does "loose electrons shared by everyone" mean? The second waits until lesson four. The first is now.

Sodium's one outer electron is on its own in a new shell. Lose it, and the shell below is full: eight electrons. Chlorine has seven in its outer shell. Gain one, and it has eight. Eight in the outer shell is the pattern of the elements that join with nothing at all, neon and argon. It is a very low place on the Hill. The pattern has a name: the octet.

The number of electrons an atom gives, takes or shares to reach it is its valency. Sodium's is one. Chlorine's is one. Oxygen's is two. Carbon's is four.

**Atoms want nothing. The arrangement with lower energy is the one that lasts, and for most atoms the lowest is a full outer shell of eight.**
