Among a million bacteria, a few carry a copying slip, from VIII.1, that changed one letter in the gene for the ribosome's drug-binding slot. The drug does not fit them. Before the drug, that change was neither help nor harm; the resistant few were a tiny, unnoticed minority.

Dose the colony, and the drug kills the ordinary bacteria and leaves the resistant ones. Stop early, and the survivors, nearly all resistant, breed back to a million. The next infection is all resistant, and the drug does nothing. The patient, and then the hospital, has a strain that the drug cannot touch. The ability of bacteria to survive a drug that once killed them is antibiotic resistance, and it is natural selection watched in a week: variation, from copying errors; inheritance, from binary division; and a drug that removes all but a few.

A full course kills nearly all, including most resistant cells, because even a resistant cell dies at a high enough dose held long enough. Fewer survivors, less selection.

No antibiotic, and the resistant share drifts or falls slightly, because the changed ribosome is a little slower. The drug is the selector. Without it, resistance has no advantage.

The mustard is the same thing with a farmer as selector. Fifty generations of choosing the leafiest, and the plant is a cabbage. Artificial selection, from VIII.5 L01, and the same rules.

**Antibiotic resistance is natural selection in a week: a rare copying slip that the drug does not fit, a drug that removes everything else, and survivors that breed. Stopping a course early breeds it. Wild mustard became cabbage the same way, with a farmer choosing.**
