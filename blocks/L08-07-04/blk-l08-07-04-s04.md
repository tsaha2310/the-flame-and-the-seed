Two billion years ago, a large cell swallowed a bacterium, from V.3, and did not digest it. The bacterium lived on inside, in the bubble of the host's wall that had wrapped it, which is why the mitochondrion has two membranes: the inner one is the bacterium's own; the outer is the host's. The bacterium burned the host's food with oxygen, far more efficiently than the host could, and passed coins back. The host gave it a safe, fed home. Both gained, from VIII.7 L01, and the deal became permanent: the bacterium's descendants are your mitochondria, and they have handed most of their genes to the nucleus over the ages, keeping a few dozen on their own small loop.

Later, in one line of those cells, the same thing happened with a photosynthesising bacterium, and its descendants are the chloroplasts of every plant.

One organism living permanently inside the cells of another, as a partner, is endosymbiosis: "living together inside". Lynn Margulis argued this in 1967 against most of her field, and the tape settled it: mitochondrial DNA sits, on the tree from VIII.6, inside one family of free-living bacteria, and chloroplast DNA inside another.

**Mitochondria and chloroplasts were free bacteria, swallowed and kept. Their two membranes, their own DNA, their bacterial ribosomes and their bacterial place on the tree are the evidence. Endosymbiosis: the oldest partnership, inside every cell of you.**
