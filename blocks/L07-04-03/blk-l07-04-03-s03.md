The fungus's threads wrap and enter the root. Where they meet, both walls are packed with carriers, from V.2. The tree hands over sugar made by its leaves, from V.10, up to a fifth of everything it makes. The fungus hands back phosphate and nitrogen compounds that its threads, far finer and further-reaching than roots, have gathered from soil the tree could never reach. A root-and-fungus partnership like this is a mycorrhiza: "fungus-root". Most plants on Earth have one, and many cannot grow in poor soil without it.

That much is well shown, in pots and in the field. It is trade, and each side does better than alone.

One fungus can wrap the roots of many plants. Labelled carbon from one tree has been found in a neighbour joined by the same fungus, and that finding gave the network its nickname: the wood wide web. How much carbon really moves this way in a real forest, whether it matters to the receiving tree, and whether anything like a message passes, is disputed. Some studies find transfers; careful reviews find them small and inconsistent. The sim marks it so.

**A mycorrhiza is a fungus wrapped into a plant's roots, trading soil minerals for the plant's sugar. Fungal networks join many plants; whether much passes between them in real forests is open science.**
