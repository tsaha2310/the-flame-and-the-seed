The bubbles that form anywhere take in a gulp of whatever the bath holds, in the bath's proportions. The cell is drinking. Any small bubble of wall pinched off inside the cell is a vesicle: a "little vessel".

The bubbles that form at pits are different. The pit's surface is lined with proteins whose pockets fit one particular molecule. That molecule sticks; nothing else does. When the pit is full of stuck molecules, it deepens and pinches off. Inside the vesicle: almost only the chosen thing, a hundred times more concentrated than the bath.

A protein on the cell's surface with a pocket shaped for one molecule is a receptor. It receives that molecule and no other, from Lock and Shape. Here, receptors are used to grab; in Book VII they are used to listen.

The cell's need for cholesterol, a fat it cannot easily make, is met this way. Blood carries cholesterol in packets; the packets fit a receptor; the pits collect them.

**A cell drinks by pinching off bubbles of the bath, and picks up chosen things by pinching off bubbles from pits lined with receptors. Both make a vesicle; only the second chooses.**
