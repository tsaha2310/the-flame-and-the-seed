Meiosis is two divisions after one copying. Before the first, every chromosome is copied, from VIII.1, and the two copies stay joined. Then each homologous pair lines up together across the cell's middle, one member from each parent, and which member goes to which side is random for each pair. The first division separates the pairs. The second separates the copies. Four gametes from one cell, each with one of every pair.

With three pairs, two choices each, eight kinds of gamete. With twenty-three pairs, two to the twenty-third: over eight million kinds, from one person, by line-up alone. Mendel's second law, independent sorting from VIII.2 L03, is this random line-up seen from outside.

And the trick. While a pair lies together before the first division, the two members swap matching stretches of DNA. A chromosome that was wholly the mother's now carries a piece of the father's, and the other way round. That exchange is crossing over. It happens a few times per pair, at random places, in every meiosis. After it, the count of distinct gametes is not eight million; it is effectively unlimited. No two gametes from one person are the same.

**Meiosis lines up each pair and sends one member to each side at random, then splits the copies: two to the twenty-third combinations. Crossing over swaps stretches between the pair's members first, so every gamete is new.**

Every seed in Ajji's jar carries a combination of its grandparents' chromosomes that has never existed before.
