A group of living things that can breed with each other and have fertile young is a species. Cabbage and cauliflower are one species; a horse and a donkey are two, because their child, the mule from the Prologue's cards, is sterile.

On the split islands, selection pushes the beaks apart, from VIII.5 L01. That alone does not make two species; big-beaked and small-beaked finches can still mate. But over thousands of generations, other differences accumulate: in courtship calls, in breeding season, in the proteins of egg and sperm, from VIII.3's fertilisation. When enough have piled up that the two do not mate, or their young are sterile, they are two species. Species form by separation and time, and the boundary is blurry for a long while.

With identical conditions and large populations, the two stay nearly the same, because the same selection acts on both. With a tiny population, something else happens: alleles rise and fall by chance alone, the way ten coin tosses give any ratio, from III.8. In a population of twenty, a common allele can vanish in a generation because the few carriers happened to die. Change in allele frequency by chance rather than selection is genetic drift. It is strongest in small populations and can push two isolated groups apart with no selection at all.

And every species that has ever lived, traced back, joins others. Finches join other birds, birds join reptiles, and so on down to a single line of cells. The tree of life has one root: common descent.

**A species is a group that can interbreed. Separation plus time, by selection or by drift, piles up differences until two groups cannot. Every species traces back to shared ancestors: common descent.**
