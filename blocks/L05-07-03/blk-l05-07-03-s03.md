The four blocks are the electron transport chain: a line of protein complexes in the inner wall, each pulling on electrons a little harder than the one before. NADH, a weak holder, hands its two electrons to the first block. The first hands them to a shuttle, the shuttle to the second, the second to a small carrier, the carrier to the third, and the third to oxygen, the strongest puller in the cell. Each hand-off is a small fall, from III.9: a tenth of a volt here, three tenths there. A one-volt drop taken in six or seven steps.

The Tug-of-War from Book I is now a machine. A line of atoms with increasing pull, and electrons sliding down it, one small win at a time.

And here is what three of the blocks do with each small fall: they push hydrogen ions across the inner wall, from the matrix into the gap between the walls. A fall coupled to a lift, from III.8, and the lift is an ion pushed uphill against its crowding. Ten hydrogen ions cross the wall for every NADH.

Oxygen, at the end, takes the electrons, joins two hydrogen ions, and becomes water. That is the water in the flame's equation, made one electron pair at a time.

**The electron transport chain is a staircase of stronger and stronger pullers ending at oxygen. Electrons from NADH fall down it in small steps, and each step pumps hydrogen ions across the inner wall.**

The coins have not been made yet. Something has been done instead: ions have been piled up on one side of a wall. That is the reason for the second wall, and it is tomorrow.
