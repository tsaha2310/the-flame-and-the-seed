The twenty-third pair are the sex chromosomes. The long one is X; the short one is Y. A girl is XX, a boy XY. Every egg carries an X, because a mother has only X to give. A sperm carries X or Y, half and half, because the father's pair separates like any other. The square has two boxes: XX and XY, equal. The father's gamete decides, at random, at fertilisation. Nothing about the mother, the timing, or the food changes it.

Y is small and carries few genes, but one of them, early in development, switches on the pathway for male organs, from VI.6's switches. Without it, the default pathway runs, and the child is female. X carries hundreds of genes that both sexes need; a boy has one copy of each, a girl two, which matters in the next module.

Sometimes a pair fails to part at the line-up, from VIII.1 L03's checkpoint, and both members go to one side. A gamete then carries two of that chromosome or none. The failure is nondisjunction. Fertilise the two-copy gamete and the child has three. For most chromosomes that does not survive; for the twenty-first, the smallest, it does, and the result is Down syndrome, with its own set of features and, usually, a full life. For the sex pair, XXY and XO children live too, with their own differences.

**The sex chromosomes are X and Y. Eggs carry X; sperm carry X or Y; the sperm decides, at random. Nondisjunction is a pair failing to part, giving a gamete with two or none, and a child with three or one.**
