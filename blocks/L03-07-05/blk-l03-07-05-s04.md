Blood carries carbon dioxide from every burning sugar in the body. Some of it joins water to make carbonic acid, a weak acid. A weak acid sits at equilibrium: some of it has given up its hydrogen ion, becoming bicarbonate, and most has not.

Add acid, and the extra hydrogen ions push the equilibrium back: bicarbonate takes them up and becomes carbonic acid. The pH barely moves. Add base, and the base takes hydrogen ions, so the equilibrium releases more from carbonic acid to replace them. The pH barely moves again.

A weak acid and its partner, held together in solution, is a buffer. It soaks up acid and base alike, within limits. Le Chatelier's principle from III.6 is the whole mechanism.

The lungs finish the job. Extra carbonic acid falls apart into carbon dioxide and water, and you breathe the carbon dioxide out. Breathe faster and blood goes slightly base. Hold your breath and it goes slightly acid.

**A buffer is a weak acid and its partner, sitting at equilibrium. Push it with acid or base, and it leans back, so the pH barely moves.**

The sea is buffered too, by the same bicarbonate. That is why it has held near pH 8 through a long history of volcanoes, and why it is still moving slowly as fires add carbon dioxide faster than rock can answer.
