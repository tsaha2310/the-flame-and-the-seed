The flat rings with nitrogen in them are called bases, because the nitrogens can take a hydrogen ion, from III.7. Five of them matter here. Two are big, two rings fused together: adenine and guanine, A and G. Three are small, one ring: cytosine, uracil and thymine, C, U and T. Thymine is uracil with one extra carbon on the ring; it turns up in two lessons.

Snap a base to the sugar on one side and a phosphate to the sugar on the other, and you have a nucleotide: base, sugar, phosphate. Three pieces, one unit. Five bases, five nucleotides.

Each base has small groups sticking off its edge: a hydroxyl here, an amine there, a carbon-oxygen double bond. Those groups can make hydrogen bonds, from II.3, and their exact positions differ from base to base. Remember that. It is the next module.

Add two more phosphates to the sugar's phosphate, and you have the crowded tail from yesterday on a nucleotide. With adenine on it, the bench calls it adenosine triphosphate. Tomorrow it gets its short name.

**A nucleotide is a base, a sugar and a phosphate. Five bases in two sizes give five nucleotides. Chain three phosphates on one and it carries a stored fall.**
