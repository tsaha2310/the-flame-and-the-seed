A signal made by a gland and carried by the blood to cells elsewhere is a hormone. The one the startle releases is adrenaline. It reaches the liver in seconds.

Adrenaline fits its receptor, from yesterday. The receptor's inside face activates an enzyme built into the wall, which takes ATP, from IV.6, and bends it into a ring: the phosphate tail closed back onto the sugar. That small ring is cyclic AMP. The enzyme makes hundreds of them per second while the receptor holds it on. Cyclic AMP is the relay's first messenger inside the cell.

Cyclic AMP fits a pocket on an enzyme that, once bent open, puts a phosphate on other enzymes, from V.5's coupling. One of those, once phosphorylated, puts a phosphate on glycogen phosphorylase, which flips into its active shape and starts clipping the sugar bank. Every step is a phosphate added and a fold changed. Meanwhile, the same phosphate-adding switches off glycogen synthase, so the bank stops taking deposits.

And in the heart, the same adrenaline, the same receptor, the same cyclic AMP, and different enzymes downstream: the muscle beats harder. One hormone, one relay, different endings in different cells, because each cell type has its own set of downstream proteins, from VI.6.

**Adrenaline is a hormone from the gland above the kidney. Its receptor makes cyclic AMP, a ring of ATP, which starts a chain of phosphate-adding shape changes that end with the sugar bank paying out. Same messenger, different cells, different responses.**
