Water enters the plant cell by osmosis, through the bilayer slowly and, much faster, through water channels: channel proteins from V.2 L02 whose tube fits a water molecule and nothing else, not even an ion. The cell swells. Its skin presses against the cellulose fence. The fence does not stretch. The pressure rises until the push outward equals the pull of osmosis inward, and water stops entering. The cell is now firm, like a football. That firmness is turgor. A leaf stands up because every cell in it is pumped tight against its fence.

The blood cell has no fence. Water enters, it swells, and it bursts.

In strong salt water, the plant cell loses water. The skin shrinks away from the fence and the inside collapses into a shrivelled bag. That is plasmolysis. The fence stays; the leaf goes limp. That was the seedling at three o'clock, when the soil was dry and the leaf was losing water to the air faster than the roots could bring it.

The cell's pumps set how salty its inside is, from yesterday, and so set which way water moves. That is why the pumps decide where the water goes.

**In a plant cell, osmosis pushes the skin against the fence until pressure stops it. That is turgor, and it is what holds a leaf up. Lose the water and the cell collapses: plasmolysis.**
