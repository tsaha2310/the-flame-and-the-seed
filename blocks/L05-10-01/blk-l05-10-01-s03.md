The acceptor is a five-carbon sugar with a phosphate tag on each end. The enzyme holds it in its pocket and bends it so that its middle carbon is ready to take a new partner, from V.4's induced fit. A carbon dioxide from the air slides in and bonds to that carbon. Six carbons now, and the molecule is unstable; it splits at once into two three-carbon pieces, each with one phosphate tag.

The enzyme is called RuBisCO, from the long name of the acceptor and the reaction. It is slow, about three carbon dioxides a second, where most enzymes do thousands. It makes mistakes: oxygen fits its pocket too, and when oxygen gets in the leaf wastes energy cleaning up. Leaves make up for its slowness by making enormous amounts of it, which is why it is the most common protein there is.

Attaching carbon dioxide is the first step of a wheel. The wheel is the Calvin cycle, after Melvin Calvin, who traced it with labelled carbon; that story is two lessons away. Like Krebs's wheel from V.7, it takes something in, lets something out, and rebuilds its own acceptor to go round again. Unlike Krebs's, it runs uphill, and spends the ATP and NADPH the light reactions filled.

**RuBisCO attaches a carbon dioxide from the air to a five-carbon sugar, which splits into two three-carbon pieces. It is slow and clumsy and there is more of it on Earth than any other protein.**

Carbon has just gone from plus four, the bottom of the scoreboard in III.3, onto a sugar. The push has started. It is not sugar yet.
