Wire the two terminals straight together, with nothing between them to do work, and you have a short circuit. The electrons still fall. The zinc still dissolves. All the free energy of the reaction comes out as heat in the wire and the water, and none of it as work. A shorted battery gets hot, drains fast, and lifts nothing.

The diya is exactly this. The oil's carbon gives electrons, the air's oxygen takes them, and the fall is steep. But there is no wire, no motor, no wheel. The electrons go straight from fuel to oxygen and every bit of the drop turns into heat and light. It is the same redox as a battery, run as a short.

The seed's sugar is the same fuel with the same fall. If the seed short-circuited it, it would cook. It does not cook. So somewhere in the seed there must be a load: a wheel, a motor, something that catches part of the fall and turns it into work before the rest becomes heat.

**A flame is a short circuit: the whole fall goes to heat. A living thing must have a load in the circuit, catching some of the fall as work.**

You do not yet know what the load is. That is Book V. But you know it must exist, because the seed is warm, not on fire.
