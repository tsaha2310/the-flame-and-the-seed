Direction first. After a spot fires, its sodium gates shut and cannot reopen for a millisecond or two, whatever the voltage. That short lockout is the refractory period. When the wave reaches a spot, the spot behind it is still locked out, so the wave can only go forward. Poke a cable in the middle and two waves run, one each way, and neither can turn back.

Speed. A thicker cable carries the flip further before it fades, so its neighbours fire sooner: thick is fast. The squid's escape nerve is a millimetre thick for that reason. But a body full of millimetre-thick nerves would have no room for anything else.

The other trick: wrap the cable in insulation. Certain cells wind their own wall round the nerve's cable, layer on layer, from V.1, leaving a bare gap every millimetre. The wrapped stretches cannot fire; the gates are covered. So the flip at one gap spreads along the insulated stretch as a fast, fading nudge and reaches the next gap still above threshold, where it refires at full strength. The wave jumps from gap to gap. The wrapping is myelin, and a myelinated cable a hundredth of a millimetre thick outruns a bare squid nerve a hundred times thicker.

**The refractory period locks a spot after it fires, so the wave runs one way. Myelin wraps the cable so the wave jumps from gap to gap, fifty times faster on a thin cable.**
