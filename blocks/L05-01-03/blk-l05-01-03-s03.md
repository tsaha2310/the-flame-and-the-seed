Each room inside a cell is a bubble of the same bilayer as the outer wall, from V.1 L01, with different chemistry sealed inside. A room like that is an organelle: a "little organ".

- The big open room the others float in: water, thick with proteins and sugars, pH about 7.2. Most of the cell's reactions run here.
- The small bag at pH 5 is an acid bag. It holds cutters that only work in acid, from III.7's idea of pH, and it takes apart worn-out molecules. Animal cells have several small ones; a plant cell has one big one.
- The folded floor with grains on it is where proteins are made. The grains are the workshops, and they get their name in Book VI; the floor is a membrane that folds the new chains and ships them.
- The bean with the folds and the warmth is a mitochondrion. It burns sugar in steps and recharges ATP. It is the power plant, and it has two membranes, an outer and an inner, which matters in V.7.
- The green disc in the leaf is a chloroplast. It catches light and makes sugar. It has two membranes too, and stacks of green sheets inside.
- The largest inner room, with the DNA inside, is the nucleus. Not the atom's nucleus from Book I. Same word, different thing, chosen because both are "the kernel".

Nobody is in charge. Molecules bump at random, from the Crowd, and shape decides what sticks, from Lock and Shape. A molecule that fits the acid bag's cutters is taken apart there. One that does not fit passes by. That is the whole of how a cell is organised.

**A cell's rooms are bubbles of the same wall with different chemistry inside: an acid bag, a workshop floor, a power plant, a green panel, a kernel of tape. Nobody is in charge; shape decides what happens where.**
