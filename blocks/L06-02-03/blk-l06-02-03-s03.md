A copy lives about an hour, then its own backbone cuts it, from IV.8 L02. So the number of copies alive at any moment is set by two rates: how fast the copyist makes them, and how fast they fade. Raise the copying rate and the copies climb to a new steady level in about an hour. Cut it, and the copies are gone in about an hour.

That is a control. A cell that stops copying a gene stops making its protein soon after, without having to hunt down and destroy anything. The decay does the work.

Compare a copy that lasted forever. To turn a protein off, the cell would have to find every copy and destroy it. To turn it down a little, it could not; it could only add more. A permanent copy is a tap with no off.

So the short life of mRNA is the third reason for copying. Safety, parallelism, and control. The tape lasts because it is DNA. The messages fade because they are RNA. Each molecule's lifetime fits its job.

**Copies wear out on their own, so how much of a protein a cell makes is set by how fast it copies the gene. Stop copying, and the protein stops. The short life of mRNA is the off switch.**
