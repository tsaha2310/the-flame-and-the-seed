Cuts per second rise with temperature, as collisions do, until about 40 degrees. Then they fall off a cliff. Above 50 the fold is gone, from IV.5: the pocket has unfolded, and there is nothing to dock into. Cool it back down and it stays dead; the chain is tangled. That is the boiled seed's enzymes, every one.

The temperature at which an enzyme works fastest is its optimum. For a human enzyme, about body temperature. For an enzyme from a hot-spring bacterium, 80 degrees. The optimum is where the fold is the best shape it can be.

pH has an optimum too. The charged side chains in the active site, from IV.5, hold the substrate and strain it. Change the pH and those charges change; the pocket loses its grip. A gut enzyme peaks near pH 8. The stomach's enzyme peaks near pH 2 and does nothing at 8.

The inhibitor bin: some small molecules fit the pocket and sit there without reacting. While one sits, the substrate cannot enter. Cuts per second fall. A molecule that jams an enzyme is an inhibitor. Many poisons are inhibitors. So are many medicines.

And one more word for what you saw in the docking bay: the pocket closing around the substrate, changing its own shape as the substrate enters, is induced fit. Induced means brought about; the fit is made, not found.

**An enzyme has an optimum temperature and pH where its fold is right. Too hot or too far in pH and the fold goes. A molecule that sits in the pocket without reacting is an inhibitor.**
