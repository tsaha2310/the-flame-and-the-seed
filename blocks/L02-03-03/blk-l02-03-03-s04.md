In the water cut-away, the negative corner of each molecule sits close to a positive hydrogen of a neighbour. Opposite partial charges, close together, low on the Hill. Every molecule is held by several neighbours.

That link, a hydrogen on one molecule pulled toward an oxygen or nitrogen on another, is called a hydrogen bond. It is not a bond in the sense of Book II's grips; it is far weaker than a shared pair. But water has two hydrogens and two lone pairs per molecule, so each one can make four, and together they are strong enough to hold thirty drops on a coin.

Polar molecules that are not so lucky, like the nail-polish remover, still line up positive to negative, but more weakly. That pull is called a dipole force.

Oil molecules have no charged sides. Yet oil is a liquid, not a gas, so something must hold its molecules together. The electrons in any molecule are always sloshing about, and for an instant one side has slightly more than the other. That instant of lopsidedness tugs on a neighbour. The tug is called a dispersion force. It is the weakest of the three, and it is all oil has.

**Molecules pull on each other through their partial charges. Water's hydrogen bonds are the strongest of these pulls, and oil has only the weakest.**

Now the ghee on the dal, honestly. Water molecules pull on each other so hard that oil is squeezed out. It is not that water pushes oil away. The oil simply cannot get between water molecules that are holding each other.
