Every copper atom has one outer electron that is held so weakly it is not really the atom's own. It drifts. So does every other copper atom's. The result is a lattice of positive copper ions sitting in a shared sea of loose electrons.

The pull between the positive ions and the sea around them is a metallic bond. No ion is bonded to one particular neighbour. The whole lump is held by the whole sea.

That is why the hammer cannot break it. Slide a row of ions along, and they are still surrounded by the same sea. Nothing repels, nothing lets go. It dents.

And that is why it conducts. The sea is free to drift, and a battery's push makes it drift one way. That drift is the current you met in Book I.

Heat makes the ions jiggle harder, which gets in the way of the drift. That is why a hot wire carries a little less current, the opposite of salt water, where heat helped the ions along.

**In a metal, the outer electrons belong to the whole lump. The ions can slide and the electrons can flow, which is why metals bend and conduct.**

Now all three grips sit on one dial: the difference in pull.

- Big difference: a steal. Ions in a lattice. Salt.
- Small difference, both pulling hard: a share. Molecules. Sugar, water, air.
- Small difference, both pulling weakly: a pool. Ions in a sea. Copper.
