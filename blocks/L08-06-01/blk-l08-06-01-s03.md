Sorting living things into nested groups by shared features is classification. Linnaeus's groups were boxes within boxes: a species inside a genus inside larger groups. His two-word names, genus then species, are binomial names: Homo sapiens, Vigna radiata for the moong, Panthera tigris. One name for every species in every language, and still in use.

Linnaeus thought he was cataloguing a fixed creation. After Darwin, the boxes turned out to be branches. Shared features are homologous structures, from VIII.5 L02, inherited from a shared ancestor, and nested groups are nested branches of descent. A classification done right is a family tree.

The trick cards show why looks can mislead. A whale lives like a fish and is built like a mammal: lungs, milk, a flipper with hand bones. A bat flies like a bird and is a mammal with a hand for a wing. A platypus lays eggs, has fur, and makes milk: a mammal from a branch that split before the egg-laying was lost. Sort by the features that are inherited, not by the features that suit a way of life, and the trick cards find their places.

**Classification is sorting by shared inherited features into nested groups, and every species gets a two-word name. After Darwin, the nested groups are branches of one tree.**
