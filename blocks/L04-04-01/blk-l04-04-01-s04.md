The piece your saliva snips off is glucose. Six carbons. Every carbon but one carries a hydroxyl, and the last carries a double-bonded oxygen. So many oxygens that its carbons average a score of zero, from III.3: half-burned fuel, as IV.2 said.

Five hydroxyls on one small molecule make it love water more than almost anything else does. Glucose dissolves instantly. It is sweet, because your tongue's sweet detectors are shaped for it, a point for Book IV's next-but-one rung.

In water, glucose spends most of its time curled into a ring: the chain's end carbon bends round and bonds to the oxygen on the fifth carbon, closing a six-sided ring with five carbons and one oxygen in it. The ring opens and closes constantly. Both are glucose.

Glucose is one member of a family. Fructose, the sugar in fruit and honey, has the same formula and a different arrangement: an isomer, with a five-membered ring. Galactose, from milk, is another isomer of glucose, differing by one hydroxyl pointing the other way. All three are sugars: small carbon molecules crowded with hydroxyls.

**A sugar is a short carbon chain crowded with hydroxyls, which is why it loves water. Glucose is the one every living thing runs on.**

The roti's long chain is glucose after glucose after glucose. What joins them, and how the saliva cuts it, is tomorrow.
