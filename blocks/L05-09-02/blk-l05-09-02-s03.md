Red is absorbed strongly. Blue is absorbed strongly. Green is mostly thrown back. That is why leaves are green: it is the leftover.

The chloroplast, from V.1's tour, has two membranes like a mitochondrion, and inside, stacks of flat discs. Each disc is a closed bubble of membrane called a thylakoid, and the discs are stacked in piles. A lot of surface area in a small room: V.1 L04's rule again.

Set in the thylakoid membranes is the molecule that does the absorbing: chlorophyll. A flat ring, like a base from IV.6 but larger, with a magnesium ion at its centre and a long oily tail that anchors it in the membrane. Its electrons sit on rungs, from I.3, spaced so that a red photon or a blue photon lifts one, and a green photon fits no gap and passes through.

An electron lifted to a higher rung is an electron pushed up the Hill. That is the push van Helmont was missing. Light does not warm the leaf into making sugar. A photon of the right colour lifts one electron in one chlorophyll, and the rest of the module is what happens to that electron before it falls back.

**Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue photons and throws back green. Each absorbed photon lifts one electron up a rung. That lift is the push that runs a plant uphill.**
