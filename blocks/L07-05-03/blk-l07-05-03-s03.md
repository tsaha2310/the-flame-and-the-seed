Animal tissues come in four kinds.

Epithelial tissue: sheets of cells packed edge to edge, covering every surface and lining every tube. The gut's lining is epithelial, with the pumps and carriers from V.2 in its cells' walls; so is your skin; so is the lining of a lung.

Connective tissue: cells scattered in a mesh of fibres they secrete, holding other tissues together and carrying blood vessels. Under the gut lining, around every organ, and, in stiffer forms, bone and cartilage; in liquid form, blood.

Muscle tissue: cells full of myosin and actin, from V.5, that shorten when a nerve tells them. Two layers round the gut, one running along and one round, squeeze food forward.

Nervous tissue: neurons and their helpers, from VII.2, wired into a net in the gut wall that times the squeezes, and into the brain and spinal cord.

Several tissues layered into a structure with a job is an organ. The stomach, with its acid-making lining that wakes pepsin, from V.4. The small gut, with its absorbing lining folded into fingers for surface area, from V.1 L04. A set of organs working on one task is an organ system: mouth to stomach to small gut to large gut is the digestive system.

**Four animal tissues: epithelial sheets, connective mesh, muscle, and nerve. Layered together they make an organ; organs in a chain make a system.**
