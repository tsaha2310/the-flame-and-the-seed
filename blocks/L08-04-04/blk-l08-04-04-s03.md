Someone with one copy of a recessive allele, who shows nothing and can pass it on, is a carrier. A harmful recessive allele hides in carriers. Selection only sees it when two carriers have a child with two copies, and in a population where the allele is rare, that is rare, so the allele lingers for many generations. A harmful dominant allele is exposed in every person who has it, and if it acts in childhood it is removed fast.

Without malaria, the sickle allele slowly falls, generation by generation, as the occasional two-copy child dies. With malaria, something else happens. Two-copy children die of sickle disease. But no-copy children die of malaria more often than carriers do, because the malaria parasite grows poorly in a carrier's red cells. Carriers survive best. And carriers must have the allele. So the allele is held at a frequency where the deaths from the two causes balance: high where malaria is fierce, low where it is absent.

The map of the sickle allele across Africa, the Mediterranean and India is a map of where malaria has been for thousands of years. A harmful allele is common because, in one copy, it protects against something worse.

**A carrier has one copy of a recessive allele and shows nothing, so harmful recessives hide for generations. The sickle allele is common where malaria is, because carriers survive malaria better than anyone.**
