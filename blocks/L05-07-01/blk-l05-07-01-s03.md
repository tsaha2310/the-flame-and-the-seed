Two bilayers, one inside the other, make three spaces: outside, a thin gap between the walls, and the innermost space. The innermost space is the matrix. Most of the burning happens there.

The inner wall is folded into deep shelves, and the folding is the surface rule from V.1 L04: the machinery of the next three lessons sits in the inner wall, and folding it gives the plant ten times the room for it.

Pyruvate arrives from glycolysis, through the outer wall, which is leaky, and then through a carrier in the inner wall, which is not. Inside the matrix a large enzyme meets it. One carbon is stripped off as carbon dioxide, the first breath the cell makes. A pair of electrons fills an NAD+. And the two-carbon piece that is left is attached to a carrier molecule called coenzyme A, giving acetyl-CoA: a two-carbon fragment on a handle, ready for the wheel.

**A mitochondrion is two walls: an outer one and a folded inner one, with the matrix inside. Pyruvate enters, loses a carbon as carbon dioxide, fills a bucket, and becomes acetyl-CoA.**

Three carbons in. One out as gas already. Two on the handle. The peanut's carbon is leaving you, one at a time.
