Three to one: the T and t chromosomes of a pair separate in meiosis, so half the gametes carry T and half t. Two such parents, random fertilisation, four equally likely combinations, three showing T. The Punnett square is a picture of gametes meeting.

Nine, three, three, one: T and R sit on different pairs. Each pair lines up on its own side at random, so a gamete gets TR, Tr, tR or tr equally. Sixteen combinations at fertilisation. Independent sorting is the independent line-up of different pairs.

T and R on the same pair, close together: they travel together, and the ratio is the bundled one, three to one with no recombinants. Far apart on the same pair with crossing over: some gametes carry a swapped stretch, and the ratio sits between bundled and independent. The fraction of recombinants measures the distance between the two genes on the chromosome. That is how Thomas Morgan's students drew the first chromosome maps in 1913, with fruit flies.

The joining of two gametes into one cell with the full count is fertilisation. In a moong, a pollen grain grows a tube down into the flower and delivers its nucleus to the egg's. The joined cell has eleven pairs again, one member of each from each parent, and divides by mitosis, from VIII.1 L03, into every cell of the seed.

**Three to one is a pair separating. Nine to three to three to one is two pairs lining up independently. Genes on one pair travel together unless crossing over parts them. Fertilisation joins two halves into a full set, and mitosis builds the seed from it.**
