Every bit of stuff holds energy in two bins. One is jiggle: how fast its molecules move, which the thermometer reads. The other is grips: the energy stored in the pulls and bonds between and inside molecules, which the thermometer cannot see. The two together are the substance's internal energy.

Pour heat into liquid water and it mostly goes to jiggle. The temperature rises.

Pour heat into ice at zero and it goes to grips. Each bit of heat loosens hydrogen bonds and lets molecules leave the lattice. Until the lattice is gone, the thermometer does not move. The same happens at 100 degrees: heat goes into pulling molecules fully apart into steam, and the temperature waits.

Chemists have a name for the heat content of a substance measured at ordinary, constant pressure, the way everything in a kitchen happens: enthalpy. When a reaction gives out heat, its enthalpy has gone down. When it takes heat in, its enthalpy has gone up. You will meet the word again on the Hill.

**Heat can go into jiggle, which the thermometer sees, or into grips, which it cannot. Melting and boiling are all grips and no jiggle.**
