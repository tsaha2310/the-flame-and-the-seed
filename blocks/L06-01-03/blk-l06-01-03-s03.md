Two metres of thread, two nanometres wide, into a room five micrometres across. Wound on spools of protein, the string is ten times shorter. Coiled, ten times shorter again. Folded and looped, it fits, with room to spare. And, because every level of packing can be undone locally, any stretch can be unwound and read while the rest stays packed.

The thread is not one piece. In a human cell it is forty-six pieces, each wound and coiled into its own body. One such packed piece of DNA, with its spools, is a chromosome. The moong has twenty-two. A bacterium has one, and it is a loop.

Along the thread, a stretch that carries the recipe for one protein, or for one RNA tool from IV.7, is a gene. Recipes are separated by stretches that carry no recipe at all. A human has about twenty thousand genes on three billion letters; most of the thread is between the genes.

All of it together, every letter on every chromosome, is the genome: the full tape of the organism.

**Two metres of DNA is wound on protein spools, coiled and folded into chromosomes that fit in a nucleus and can be unwound one stretch at a time. A gene is one recipe on the tape. The genome is all of it.**

"A recipe, not a drawing" is the sentence to keep. A gene does not draw a protein or a trait. It lists the beads in order. What the finished thing looks like depends on the fold, the cell, and what else is going on.
