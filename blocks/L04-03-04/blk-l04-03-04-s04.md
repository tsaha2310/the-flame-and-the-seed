In plain water, soap molecules gather with their tails together and their heads outward: a tiny ball with an oily inside and a watery outside. Water's molecules squeeze the tails into the middle, as II.3 said they would, and the charged heads sit happily among the water. The ball is a micelle.

Add grease, and the tails find it. They burrow into the grease drop with their heads still out in the water. The grease becomes the inside of a micelle. Now the drop has a charged, water-loving surface, and the water carries it away as easily as it carries salt.

This is soap: a salt of a fatty acid, one end charged and one end oily, and its trick is to give oil a watery coat. Nobody planned it. Water pulls on water so hard that oily tails are pushed together, and charged heads are pulled apart. The shape of the micelle falls out of the pulls.

**A molecule with a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail arranges itself: tails in, heads out. That is a micelle, and it is how soap carries oil away.**

Hold on to this picture. In Book V a molecule with two loves and two tails will do the same thing, and the result will not be a ball. It will be a wall.
