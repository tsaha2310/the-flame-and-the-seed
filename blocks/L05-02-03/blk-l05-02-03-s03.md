A protein that moves something uphill, from where it is scarce to where it is crowded, is a pump. It cannot do this for free: uphill costs, from the Hill. The pump pays with ATP. It binds three sodium ions inside and an ATP; the ATP's end phosphate comes off onto the pump, from IV.6; the pump bends, flips, and drops the three sodium outside. There it binds two potassium ions, drops the phosphate, flips back, and drops the potassium inside. Three out, two in, one coin. That is the sodium-potassium pump, and it is coupling, from III.8, with the pump as the wheel.

The result is a gradient: a difference in concentration across the wall, held there against the leaks. Sodium is ten times more crowded outside than in. Potassium is thirty times more crowded inside than out.

And because three charges go out for every two that come in, the inside ends up slightly negative. The charge meter reads about a tenth of a volt, inside negative. That is a voltage across a wall, from III.9's definition: a difference, and a push. A gradient is a charged battery, made of ions instead of metals.

**A pump spends ATP to push ions uphill against the leaks. The gradient it builds is a stored fall, a battery across the wall.**

The pump never stops. Turn off the coins and the gradient leaks away in minutes, and the cell is levelled.
