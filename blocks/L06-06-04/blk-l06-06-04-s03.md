In a plant or animal cell the copyist cannot land on a promoter alone. It needs a set of proteins that grip short DNA sequences near the gene and, by their shapes, help or hinder its landing, from Lock and Shape. A protein that binds DNA and changes how much a gene is copied is a transcription factor. Some help, some hinder. A gene's copying rate is set by which factors are present and bound.

Some of the sequences a factor grips are far from the promoter, thousands of letters away. The DNA loops so that a factor bound there touches the copyist at the promoter. Such a distant control sequence is an enhancer. One gene can have several, each read by different factors, so a gene can be on in muscle, off in nerve, and on again in a wound.

Above all of that sits the chromatin, from VI.5 L01. A region wound tight is unreadable whatever factors are around. A cell's identity is largely which regions are loose and which are tight, set during development and mostly kept for the cell's life.

A muscle cell: muscle regions loose, muscle factors present, nerve regions tight. A nerve cell: the reverse. Housekeeping genes, for glycolysis, ribosomes and the like, loose in both.

**Transcription factors grip DNA near a gene and set its copying rate. Enhancers are distant control sequences the DNA loops to reach. Chromatin, loose or tight, decides which genes can be switched at all. A cell type is a switch setting.**
