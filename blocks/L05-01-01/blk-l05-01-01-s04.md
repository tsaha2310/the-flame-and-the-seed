Soap's one tail lets its molecules pack into a ball with tails pointing to a centre. Two tails are too wide for that. They cannot fit around a point; they fit side by side in a sheet. So the two-tailed molecules line up shoulder to shoulder, tails together, heads facing the water. And because tails would be exposed at the edge of a single layer, a second layer lies against the first, tails to tails. That is a bilayer: two layers, heads out on both faces, an oily middle two tails thick.

A flat bilayer still has an exposed edge, so the sheet curls until the edges meet and it becomes a closed bubble with water inside and water outside. Nothing pushed it. Water molecules pull on each other so hard that oily tails are squeezed out of sight, and the bubble is the shape that hides them.

The molecule is a phospholipid: a fat's glycerol with two fatty acid tails and a phosphate on the third position, from IV.3 and IV.6. The phosphate head is charged and loves water. The tails do not.

**A fat with a phosphate head and two tails makes a two-layer sheet in water, tails in, heads out, and the sheet closes into a bubble on its own. That bubble is the wall.**

The wall built itself. That is the first sentence of Book V, and it will come back in the Epilogue.
