Add reactant, and the left room is crowded. More cross left-to-right than before. The split shifts toward products until the rates balance again. The equilibrium has moved, partly undoing the push.

Remove product, and fewer cross back. The split shifts toward products again. Keep removing product, and the reaction keeps running to replace it. That is how a reaction that stops at a fifty-fifty split can be driven all the way: keep taking the product away.

Heat: whichever direction takes in heat is favoured. Gas dissolving in water gives out heat, so heating pushes the gas out. The warm bottle foams.

Squeeze a gas: whichever direction makes fewer gas molecules is favoured. Squeezing pushes carbon dioxide into the water, which is how the drink was made in the first place.

Henri Le Chatelier wrote the rule in 1884. Push an equilibrium, and it shifts in the direction that partly undoes the push. It is called Le Chatelier's principle.

**Push an equilibrium and it leans back. Add something, it uses some up. Remove something, it makes more. Heat it, it soaks heat. Squeeze it, it shrinks.**

The principle is not a force. It is only the two rooms: a push changes one crossing rate more than the other, and the split moves until they match again.
