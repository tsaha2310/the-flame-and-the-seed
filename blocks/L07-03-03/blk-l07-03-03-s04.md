In fast flow, the signal each cell leaks is carried away at once. The concentration at the surface stays low, the switch stays off, and the cells live as loners.

In still water, or in a sheltered crevice between teeth, the signal builds. Past threshold, the switch flips, and among the genes it turns on are ones for making a sticky coat of sugars and proteins, from IV.4's chains. Each cell secretes it, the coats merge, and the colony becomes a slime-covered mat glued to the surface. A community of bacteria living in a shared slime on a surface is a biofilm. Plaque is one. So is the slime on a stone in a stream, and the film inside a hospital tube.

Inside a biofilm, the cells are protected. Antibiotics diffuse in slowly, from III.5, and the cells at the bottom grow slowly and are hard to kill. That is why plaque must be scraped, not rinsed, and why a catheter infection is hard to clear.

Bassler showed that many bacteria speak several signal molecules, some for their own kind and one, shared, that seems to count every bacterium present, of any species. A chemical census.

**Flow washes signal away; still water lets it build. Past threshold, bacteria switch on genes for a shared slime and become a biofilm, protected and stuck. Plaque is a quorum reached in a crevice.**
