The oxygen carrier of blood is haemoglobin: four chains, each with an iron-holding ring, packed in every red blood cell. The mutation changes one letter in one codon of one chain, GAG to GUG, and one glutamate becomes one valine. Minus to oily, on the surface, from IV.5 L05.

The protein folds. It carries oxygen. In oxygen-rich blood, nothing seems wrong. When the blood gives up its oxygen in a working muscle, the protein changes shape a little, and in that shape the new oily patch is exposed. Water squeezes it against the patch on the next copy, from II.3, and the copies stack into long stiff rods. The rods bend the red cell from a disc into a crescent, a sickle. Sickled cells are stiff, jam in narrow vessels, and break. That is sickle cell disease: pain, damage, and a short life without treatment.

One letter. Millions of people carry the changed spelling. Why so common a harmful letter has not disappeared is a Book VIII question with a surprising answer.

**One letter, GAG to GUG, puts an oily bead on haemoglobin's surface. In low oxygen the copies stack into rods that sickle the red cells. A missense mutation, from Book IV's swap, in a real protein.**
