A hydrogen ion and a hydroxide ion meet and join into water. The acid's sourness and the base's slipperiness cancel. This is neutralisation.

What is left is the acid's other half and the base's other half, as ions in the water. Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide leave sodium ions and chloride ions: table salt. Any acid plus any base leaves water plus one of these ion pairs. Chemists call every such ion pair a salt, in a wider sense than the kitchen's. Nimbu and chuna make calcium citrate, a salt. Vinegar and baking soda make sodium acetate, a salt, with carbon dioxide fizzing off.

The dripping-in to find the exact point of cancellation is a titration. The indicator flips colour on the drop where the hydrogen ions run out. From the volume of base it took, and the recipe, you get the acid's concentration to two decimal places. It is the balance from Book I, done with a colour change instead of a pan.

**Acid plus base gives water plus a salt. A titration finds the exact drop where they cancel, and from it, how strong the acid was.**
