Sodium's outer electron is far from the nucleus and shielded by two full shells. It is high on the Hill. Pulling it off costs little. Chlorine's outer electrons are close in, the nucleus has 17 protons pulling, and they are low on the Hill. Pulling one off costs a great deal.

The cost of removing an outer electron is called ionisation energy. Low for sodium, high for chlorine.

The other side is how much an atom gains when it takes an extra electron. That is electron affinity. Chlorine gains a lot: one more electron fills its shell, a fall down the Hill. Sodium gains almost nothing.

Put the two together and you get a single score: how hard an atom pulls on electrons it shares. That score is electronegativity. Fluorine is the strongest puller in the whole table. Oxygen and chlorine are close behind. Sodium and potassium barely pull at all.

- Across a row, left to right: atoms get smaller, and the pull rises.
- Down a column: atoms get bigger, and the pull falls.

**Two atoms pulling on shared electrons: the stronger puller wins some or all. This is the Tug-of-War, and it decides every bond in the seed and the flame.**

Now the honest version. Atoms do not want or try. A stronger pull just means the electron sits lower on the Hill near that nucleus, and low is where things end up.
