Every generation, the beetles differ a little from each other in colour. That spread of differences among individuals is variation, and it comes from copying errors and shuffling, from VIII.1 and VIII.3, and is inherited, from VIII.2.

You ate the ones you saw first. On sand, the dark ones. The pale ones bred, passed on pale, and in twenty generations the population was pale. On bark, the reverse. You did not make any beetle paler. You removed the dark ones before they bred, and the next generation was drawn from what was left.

When the chooser is a breeder picking which pigeons to pair, that is artificial selection. When the chooser is a predator, a drought, a disease, or a mate, with no one intending anything, that is natural selection. Three things make it inevitable: individuals vary; the variation is inherited; and more are born than survive to breed. Given those three, the population shifts toward whatever survives, every generation, without anyone choosing.

With mutation at zero, the beetles converge on one colour and then the chart freezes: on a new background you can eat them all. With mutation high, the chart smears and the population never matches any background well. With no hunting, the chart drifts and spreads: variation without selection is just variation.

**Natural selection: individuals vary, the variation is inherited, and more are born than survive, so whatever survives becomes commoner. Nobody chooses. A predator, a drought or a mate does the removing.**

Two sentences to keep, honestly. Mutations are random. Selection is not. Nothing evolves because it needs to.
