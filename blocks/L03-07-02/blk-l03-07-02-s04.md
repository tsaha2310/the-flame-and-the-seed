Each ten-fold dilution raises the pH by exactly one. pH 1 to pH 2 to pH 3. The count of loose hydrogen ions falls to a tenth each time.

pH is a way of writing the hydrogen ion concentration as a small number. Each step is ten times. pH 2 has ten times the hydrogen ions of pH 3, a hundred times pH 4, a million times pH 8. Pure water sits at 7, where the tiny number of hydrogen ions equals the tiny number of hydroxide ions. Below 7 is acid. Above 7 is base.

So nimbu at 2.4 and vinegar at 2.8 are not close. And the gap between the chemist's acid at 1 and vinegar at 2.8 is not one step, it is sixty times.

Vinegar at the same concentration as the chemist's acid reads a higher pH, meaning fewer loose hydrogen ions. The cut-away shows why. The chemist's hydrochloric acid gives up every hydrogen it has: a strong acid. Vinegar's acetic acid holds most of its hydrogens and gives up only a few: a weak acid. It is the two rooms from III.6. The weak acid sits at an equilibrium with most of its hydrogens still attached.

**Each step of pH is ten times. A strong acid gives up all its hydrogen ions. A weak one holds most back, at equilibrium.**
