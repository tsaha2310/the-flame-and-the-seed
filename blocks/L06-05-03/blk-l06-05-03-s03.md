The splicing machine recognises boundary signs, but which boundaries it uses can be steered. Proteins in the nucleus can hide an exon's sign, or strengthen it, so that the machine skips that exon or includes it. Different cells, or the same cell at different times, splice one gene's copy in different ways. That is alternative splicing.

Skip an exon, and a stretch of beads is missing from the chain. From IV.5, the fold changes, and with it the job. One fold might sit in a membrane; a shorter one might float free. One might bind a partner; one might not. One gene, several proteins, related but different.

Most human genes are spliced in more than one way. That is how twenty thousand genes make over a hundred thousand proteins, and part of why a gene count says little about how complex a living thing is.

**One gene can be spliced in several ways, keeping different sets of exons, to give several different proteins. Twenty thousand genes make far more than twenty thousand proteins.**

The introns that looked like junk yesterday are what make this possible: they are the seams along which a recipe can be recut.
