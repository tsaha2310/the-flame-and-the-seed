The two-carbon acetyl piece is handed onto a four-carbon molecule already on the wheel, making six. Round the wheel, two carbons are stripped off as carbon dioxide, and the four-carbon molecule is rebuilt to take the next acetyl. The wheel is the Krebs cycle, after Hans Krebs, who worked it out in 1937 by feeding labelled pieces to minced pigeon muscle.

On the way round, carbons are stripped of electrons again and again, from III.3: three NAD+ are filled, one other carrier is filled, and one coin is made directly. The carbons that leave as gas are fully oxidised, at plus four. This is where the peanut's carbon becomes breath.

The labelled carbons do not leave on the first turn. The two carbon dioxides of the first turn come from the four-carbon part. Your red carbons leave on the second and third turns. The wheel does not care which carbon is which; it strips whichever is in position.

**The Krebs cycle takes each two-carbon acetyl round an eight-step wheel, strips its electrons into carriers, and lets the carbons go as carbon dioxide. Three buckets, one coin, per turn.**

Count so far, per glucose: two coins from glycolysis, two from the wheel. Four of thirty. But ten buckets are full, and those are the twenty-six.
