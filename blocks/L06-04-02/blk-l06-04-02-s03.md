The machine is a ribosome. Two subunits, a small one that holds the mRNA and checks codons, and a large one that joins beads. Both are mostly RNA, folded like IV.7's hairpins into a shape, with proteins tucked around the outside.

Three slots, side by side, each holding one tRNA against one codon. The right-hand slot takes a new charged tRNA, and the small subunit checks that its anticodon pairs with the codon there. The middle slot holds the tRNA carrying the chain so far. The joining happens between them: the chain is handed from the middle tRNA's hook onto the bead on the right-hand tRNA, by a peptide bond, from IV.5 L02. Then the ribosome shifts one codon along: the right-hand tRNA, now carrying the chain, moves to the middle; the middle tRNA, now empty, moves to the left-hand slot and leaves. The right-hand slot is empty again.

The joining is done by the large subunit's RNA, not by a protein. Ramakrishnan, with Ada Yonath and Thomas Steitz, mapped this atom by atom by 2000. The peptide bond, the bond that makes every protein, is made by RNA. The Tape is a machine, from IV.7 L03, at the very centre of the cell.

Twenty beads a second, in a bacterium. At a stop codon no tRNA fits; a protein slips in instead, the chain is released, and the two subunits let go.

**A ribosome is two RNA-rich subunits with three tRNA slots. Check the pairing, join the beads, shift one codon. The joining is done by RNA. The Tape builds the machines.**
