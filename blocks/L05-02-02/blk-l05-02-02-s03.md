A protein sits in the wall if it has an oily belt where it touches the tails, from IV.5's folding rule. Polar all over, and water squeezes it out of the wall.

A protein with a polar tube through its middle is a channel protein. Water and small ions can pass down the tube without touching the oily tails. The tube chooses by width and by lining. A narrow tube with a minus lining lets positive ions through and repels negative ones, from I.2. A tube of a certain width fits potassium and not the slightly smaller, more heavily water-wrapped sodium. Real channels are that choosy.

A channel is a road, not a pump. What goes through goes downhill: from where it is crowded to where it is not, by diffusion, from III.5. A channel cannot push anything uphill.

A carrier protein is a different design. It has a pocket that fits one molecule, from Lock and Shape. When the molecule sits in the pocket, the protein's fit bends it, and it flips to face the other side of the wall. The molecule falls out. Then the carrier flips back. Glucose enters cells this way: one at a time, downhill, only where there is more outside than in.

**A channel is a shaped tube through the wall; a carrier is a shaped pocket that flips. Both are choosy, and both only move things downhill.**

Cholera's toxin holds a chloride channel in the gut open. Chloride pours into the gut, water follows by osmosis, and the patient loses litres a day.
