A sex cell, pollen or sperm or egg, is a gamete. If gametes carried the full count, every fertilisation would double it, and in a few generations the nucleus could not hold the thread. So a gamete carries half: one chromosome from each pair.

The two chromosomes of a pair carry the same genes in the same order, one from each parent, but not always the same spellings. Mendel's T and t are on such a pair. A matching pair is a homologous pair: "same-shaped".

The dance that makes gametes halves the count by separating the pairs. Each gamete gets one of each pair, chosen at random, never two of one and none of another. That dance is meiosis: "lessening". Fertilisation, tomorrow's module explains, joins two halves and restores the pairs.

Mendel's first law is meiosis seen from outside. A plant carries two factors because it has two chromosomes of each pair. It gives one at random because meiosis puts one of each pair into each gamete.

**A gamete carries one chromosome from each homologous pair, half the full count. Meiosis is the division that makes it, and fertilisation restores the pairs. Mendel's pairs of factors are pairs of chromosomes.**
