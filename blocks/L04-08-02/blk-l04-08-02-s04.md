RNA's sugar is ribose. It carries a hydroxyl on its second carbon, one atom away from the phosphate link that holds the chain together. That hydroxyl is close enough to swing round and attack the link, cutting the backbone. Water finishes the job: a hydrolysis, from IV.4. At body temperature, a long RNA strand cuts itself somewhere within hours.

DNA's sugar is deoxyribose: ribose with that hydroxyl removed, a hydrogen in its place. Deoxy means "one oxygen fewer". With no hydroxyl next to the link, there is nothing to attack it. The backbone survives days, years, and, dry and cold, tens of thousands of years. Scientists have read DNA from bones older than the last ice age.

One more difference. DNA uses thymine where RNA uses uracil, the T from IV.6, uracil with an extra carbon. The reason is a repair story for Book VIII; the short version is that the extra carbon helps the seed's repair machinery tell a real T from a damaged C.

**RNA's sugar carries a hydroxyl that cuts its own backbone within hours. DNA's sugar lacks it, and DNA lasts for years. One oxygen is the difference between a message and an archive.**
