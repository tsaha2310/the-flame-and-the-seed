Zinc gives electrons more easily than copper, from the metal ladder. In the beaker, zinc atoms give up electrons and dissolve as zinc ions. But the electrons cannot reach the copper ions through the water. They go the only way they can: along the wire, through the LED, to the copper strip. There, hydrogen ions from the water, or copper ions if there are any, take them.

A redox reaction has been split into two halves, happening in two places. Zinc losing electrons at one strip. Something gaining them at the other. Each is a half-reaction. The strips are electrodes: the places where electrons enter or leave the metal. And the whole arrangement, two half-reactions joined by a wire and a liquid, is an electrochemical cell. That is what a battery is: one or more of these in a box.

The LED lit because electrons were flowing through it. A redox reaction, which in the nail-in-copper-sulphate glass only warmed the water a little, has been made to do work on the way.

**A battery is a redox reaction cut in half, with a wire between the halves. The electrons must take the wire, and on the way they can do work.**

Copper on both sides: nothing. No difference in pull, no push, no current. The two halves must be different.
