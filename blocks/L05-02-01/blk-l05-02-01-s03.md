Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross in an instant. They are small and uncharged, and they dissolve into the oily middle and out the other side.

Water crosses, more slowly. It is small, and though it is polar it has no full charge, so a little slips through the tails.

Alcohol crosses fast: small and oily enough.

Glucose barely crosses. It is bigger, and crowded with hydroxyls that hydrogen-bond to water and hate the oily middle.

Sodium and chloride ions do not cross at all in the time you waited. A charge with its water shell cannot enter oil, from III.5. A protein has no chance.

How easily a substance crosses a wall is its permeability. The bilayer is highly permeable to small uncharged molecules, slightly permeable to water, and nearly impermeable to ions and anything large.

**A bare bilayer lets gases and water through, slows sugar, and stops ions dead. The wall's oily middle decides, by the same rule as oil and water.**

That is a problem. A cell needs ions inside and sugar inside, and the wall stops both. Something in the wall must let them through. Tomorrow.
