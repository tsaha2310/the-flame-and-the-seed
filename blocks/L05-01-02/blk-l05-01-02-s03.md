Hooke's cork was dead: empty boxes, the walls that plant cells leave behind. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a cloth merchant in Holland who ground his own lenses, looked at pond water eleven years later and saw the boxes alive: swimming, spinning, dividing. He scraped his teeth and found more, and wrote to London that there were more animals in his mouth than people in the Netherlands.

By 1839 two Germans, Schleiden and Schwann, had looked at enough plants and animals to say it plainly. Every living thing is made of cells. A cell is the smallest unit that is alive. And, added later, by Virchow, with Pasteur's rule from the Prologue: every cell comes from a cell. That is cell theory.

A cell is a bubble, from yesterday, with things inside. The moong root's boxes are cells, each wrapped in a phospholipid bilayer, with a cellulose fence outside that. Kabir's blood discs are cells with no fence.

Two kinds. The pond water's smallest swimmers, and the things from Leeuwenhoek's teeth, are bacteria: one bubble, small, with no inner rooms. Those are prokaryotes. The moong's cells, and Kabir's, are much larger and hold smaller bubbles inside them, rooms within the room. Those are eukaryotes. The prefixes mean "before the kernel" and "true kernel", and the kernel is a room you meet tomorrow.

**Every living thing is made of cells, every cell comes from a cell, and a cell is a self-built bubble with chemistry inside. Bacteria are one bubble; plants and animals are bubbles with rooms.**

Two lessons have used the word "nucleus". Book I's was the centre of an atom. Tomorrow's is a room inside a cell. Same word, two things, an accident of history.
