Wax gives about 42 kilojoules per gram. Charcoal about 33. Sugar about 16.

The scoreboard explains it. In a hydrocarbon, every carbon is surrounded by hydrogens and other carbons, none of which pull harder than it. Its score is low, around minus 2, and in methane, minus 4. After burning, every carbon is in carbon dioxide at plus 4. That is a fall of six or eight points per carbon. Nothing else on the shelf falls as far.

Charcoal's carbons start at zero. A fall of four. Less than wax.

Sugar's carbons already have oxygens on them. Their average score is zero, and the oxygens they carry are dead weight in the gram. Less fall, and less carbon per gram to fall. Sugar is a fine fuel; it is just already partly burned.

A substance that can fall a long way by joining oxygen is a fuel. Carbon rich in hydrogen is carbon with a long way to fall.

Hydrocarbons are also oily for the reason Book II gave. Carbon and hydrogen pull nearly the same, so there are no partial charges, so nothing to mix with water. Oil floats on dal.

**A fuel is carbon with a long way to fall. The more hydrogen on the carbon, the lower its score, and the more heat per gram when it burns.**

That is why fat holds twice the energy of sugar, gram for gram. Fat is mostly hydrocarbon chain. Sugar is half oxygen already.
