When the two atoms pull almost equally, the knot sits in the middle. Neither wins. The two electrons, one from each atom, are shared between them and belong to both. Each atom counts the shared pair as its own, and both outer shells reach eight, or two for hydrogen.

This shared pair is a covalent bond. It joins exactly two atoms, and it holds them tightly, closer than the ions in a lattice.

When one atom pulls far harder, the knot goes all the way across. That is the steal from lesson two, and it makes ions.

In between, the knot sits off-centre: shared, but unevenly. That case gets a whole module, II.3, and it is the reason water is water.

So, sugar. Every atom in sugar is carbon, hydrogen or oxygen, and none of those pulls hard enough to steal from the others. All its bonds are shared pairs. Drop sugar in water and the molecules float apart from each other, whole, with no charge anywhere. Nothing moves in the circuit.

**When two atoms pull about equally, they share a pair of electrons. That shared pair is a covalent bond, and a molecule is atoms held by them.**

Two atoms can share more than one pair. Oxygen shares two pairs with another oxygen: a double bond. Nitrogen shares three with nitrogen: a triple bond, one of the strongest grips in nature, which is why the air's nitrogen sits there doing almost nothing.
